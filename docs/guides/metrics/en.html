Manuscript measurements

These formulas describe vocabulary variety in different ways. Compare chapters of similar length and narrative voice.

Analyzed word tokens

Words in analyzed prose, including preamble, lists and blockquotes. Paragraph profiles can cover less text.

Average words per sentence

Average words per detected sentence. Describes length, not suspense or narrative pace.

One 8-word sentence and one 16-word sentence average 12 words per sentence. The distribution also shows how often short and long sentences occur.

TTR

Distinct word forms divided by counted words. Longer passages often lower the ratio; compare similar lengths and topics.

Yule’s K

Higher K = more dominance by frequent word forms; K = 0 if each form appears once. Length and context affect comparisons; not a quality score.

HD-D

Expected share of distinct word forms in a 42-word draw: 0.5 means 21 different forms on average. Calculated exactly, not by sampling. Topic and how words are counted still affect comparisons.

HD-D needs at least 100 words.

Guiraud R

Different word forms divided by the square root of the word count (V/√N). The same vocabulary gives a lower score in a longer passage; compare similar lengths and topics.

MTLD

Estimates the run length before repeated word forms bring the variety ratio to 0.72 or below, averaging both reading directions. A partial final run counts proportionally; higher means longer varied runs. The 100-word minimum does not guarantee reliability.

MATTR

Average share of different word forms in overlapping 50-word windows. Higher means more forms per window; comparisons need the same window size and comparable topics.

Maas a²

Relates vocabulary size to text length. Lower values mean less concentration on repeated word forms under this formula; compare similar text contexts. Short inputs are unavailable.

Flesch

Language-specific formula using words per sentence and estimated syllables per word. Higher = easier reading in this model; not a quality or comprehension test.

LIX

Average sentence length plus the percentage of words longer than 6 characters. Higher = longer sentences or words; not an age or quality rating.

Long words

Share of counted words longer than the configured long-word threshold. This is one input to LIX, not the LIX score itself. Word length does not establish difficulty or quality.

Flagged passages

Paragraphs with cues of a tense switch or mixture. A heuristic for review in context.

Read the paragraphs on either side of a change. A tense shift may belong to a flashback or a quotation. The check covers tense patterns only.

Notes from severity

0 = neutral, 1 = watch, 2 = noticeable, 3 = strong difference (switch and mixture). A heuristic, not an error verdict.

Tense-hint threshold: 1 = all levels, 2 = default, 3 = strongest deviations only.

Grammatical tense & shifts

Dominant tense from language markers: past, present, mixed or neutral. A heuristic, not a grammatical verdict.

Neutral: too few tense markers for a statement.

Sentence rhythm

Longer bars mean longer sentences on average. Open two chapters with different bar lengths and read a paragraph from each aloud. How do dialogue, action and paragraph breaks affect the pace?

Sentence length variation

Variation in sentence lengths relative to their mean. 0 = equal lengths; higher = more variation. Not a quality rating.

Adjacent sentence overlap

Share of adjacent sentence pairs in the same paragraph that share at least one content word.

Repeated sentence starts

Count of runs where 3 or more consecutive sentences in the same paragraph begin with the identical word. Marks candidates for either deliberate anaphora or unintended sentence-start monotony.

Ending cues

0–3 heuristic: one point each for a closing sentence of 1–8 words, terminal ?, ! or …, and detected quoted speech. This is not a validated suspense or quality measure.

Starter entropy

Variety of the first counted word of detected sentences. Repeated openings such as 'I' lower this value; varied openings raise it. Repetition can be intentional.

I-starts

Share of detected sentences whose first counted word is 'I', including recognized contractions. 50% means half start this way; it can be an intended narrative voice.

Sentence lengths

Each row shows a sentence-length group, how many sentences are in it and their share of all sentences. Read a paragraph aloud and listen for shifts in length.

The four groups contain up to 6, 7 to 15, 16 to 25, and more than 25 words per sentence.

Share of short sentences

Share of detected sentences with 6 words or fewer. Sentence length alone does not measure scene pace.

Long sentences

Share of detected sentences longer than 25 words. Measures length, not syntactic nesting.

Sentences with many function words

The overview shows the fraction of detected sentences that match the filter. An individual sentence shows its own share of function words.

Articles and pronouns are examples of function words. Open a sentence and read the paragraph it belongs to.

Share of sentences with many function words

The filter selects sentences with at least 8 words and at least 45% function words.

Five out of eight words is 62.5%. Even a simple sentence can match, as in “In the city, we had seen him yesterday.” Read it in context and check whether the train of thought and references are clear.

Function words

Share of words on the language-specific function-word list, such as articles and pronouns. Higher = more listed words, not less meaning.

Can you tell what words like “she”, “that” or “it” refer to here?

Quotes and marked speech

Language or project settings determine which marks Lixity recognizes. The bars show the word share for each chapter.

20% means about one word in five falls inside a recognized quote or marked speech. Read the passage to see who is speaking, thinking or being quoted.

Words in quotes and marked speech

Share of words entirely inside recognized quotes or marked speech. Titles or thoughts can use the same marks.

Read the passage aloud. If a character is speaking, does the wording fit them and the moment?

Short spans (≤ 12 words)

Share of recognized spans with 1–12 words. Read the passage to see whether anyone is speaking.

Long spans (≥ 80 words)

Recognized spans with 80 or more words. Read extended monologues or explanatory passages in context.

Sentence length difference

Words per sentence in recognized quotes and marked speech minus words per sentence in the unambiguous surrounding text. A negative value indicates shorter sentences in marked passages. The comparison needs usable sentences from both.

Dialogue tags

Marks patterns such as “said” or “asked” near recognized quotes and marked speech.

Read the marked passage with its speech tag. Can you tell who is speaking?

Indirect speech

Lixity highlights cues for reported speech or thought outside recognized quotations.

Read this sentence with the one before it. Can you tell whose words or thought are being reported?

Word patterns & quotes

Pattern counts are scaled to 1,000 words to make chapters of different lengths easier to compare. Read the passage to see what the matched word does there.

Perception filters

Matches to the configured pattern for perception, thoughts and memories per 1,000 words. Read the passage to see whose perception or thought the words express.

Can you tell who is noticing, remembering or thinking here?

Sensory density

Matches to the configured sensory pattern per 1,000 words. Read the passage to see what the character or reader can hear, see or feel.

What concrete impression comes through here, for example a sound or smell?

Modals

Modal-word markers per 1,000 words. Does not distinguish meanings such as possibility and obligation or measure certainty.

Can you tell from the passage what is possible, required or allowed, and for whom?

Noun endings

Matches to the configured nominal-style pattern per 1,000 words. The default looks for endings such as -tion, -ment and -ness. Read the passage to see what action or quality the noun names.

If a noun sums up an action, can you still tell what happens?

Passive patterns

Passive-related word patterns per 1,000 words. A heuristic: adjectival participles and other uses can match; some passive forms are missed.

If there is a passive construction, check whether the reader needs to know who acts.

Adjective endings

Words with selected adjective endings per 1,000 words. A heuristic: other endings are missed and other word classes can match.

Motifs & repetition

A project motif counts matches to a saved search pattern. The repeat search finds recurring word sequences. Read each passage to see whether a phrase such as “at the window” acts as a motif, sets the scene or repeats unintentionally.

Word echoes

Lixity counts each occurrence of a word of four or more characters that repeats in the same paragraph and is outside the function-word list. Case is ignored.

Read each occurrence in turn. Is the repetition meant to connect the passages or emphasize an idea?

Character name mentions

Counts names in prose, not presence in a scene. Name patterns can overlap, so one mention may count for multiple figures. POV chapters are declared in the project.

Enter the names or name patterns in the project. When a name drops out for several chapters, check whether the character is still referred to with pronouns.

Internal gap

Longest run of chapters without a mention between the first and last mentions.

Mention coverage

Share of all chapters in which the name is mentioned at least once.

Character voices vs. narrator

Deterministic single-speaker adjacent-window attribution (ambiguous turns skipped). Descriptive comparison, not a style score.

Character interactions

Deterministic adjacent-turn speaker exchanges and shared chapter co-occurrence. Descriptive signal, not a quality score.

Scenes & style registers

Each scene is measured separately. Registers and target ranges are project choices, not quality judgments. Chapters serve as scenes when no dividers are present. Set assignments and targets in lixity.toml.

Word and sentence counts show the sample size. HD-D is unavailable below 100 words; sentence variation needs at least two sentences. Standard errors are approximations, not validated confidence intervals. A dash means unavailable; even an observed zero does not establish certainty.

Purely descriptive transition cues (time, place, character) inside explicit scenes; not a structural prescription.

Style

Compare a chapter with typical values from measurable chapters of the same manuscript. Start with the raw value and the amount of text measured. A chapter written as a letter may deliberately sound different from the surrounding narrative.

By default, each measure needs at least 2 measurable chapters. If every value is identical, there is no spread to use for comparison.

A plus means higher than typical for this manuscript. A minus means lower. Select a cell to see the values in their own units, such as words per sentence, and read the chapter.

Style X-ray (Heatmap)

Chapter features compared with the manuscript median, adjusted for spread and estimated measurement uncertainty. Blue = below, orange = above. Click for an explanation and access to the chapter. No comparison (–) below 100 words, or below 5 sentences for sentence-distribution features.

Style fingerprint

Each feature’s median describes a typical chapter in this manuscript. Differences can reflect intentional changes in voice or register.

Style reference

Descriptive manuscript style bands (median ± 2σ). Intentional voice and register changes may differ; not writing requirements.

Comparisons within the limit

Share of measurable chapter-feature comparisons within the chosen deviation limit. Not a quality score. Insufficient data: –.

Feature-rank projection

Exploratory projection of standardized feature ranks for complete chapters. Proximity applies to the displayed axes. Variance shares come from the regularized rank matrix, not established literary registers. Incomplete chapters are omitted.

Style across chapters

Compare style features across chapters and between the two manuscript halves. Finding candidate changes requires a complete chapter series. Trends and half comparisons can use available values. Signals help you choose chapters to reread.

FDR

When many chapter features are compared, a nominal multiple-testing rule marks a shortlist for review. Read these signals with their size and prose context; chapter error rates are not calibrated.

Chapter matrix

Each row summarizes one chapter. A dash indicates that a comparison value is unavailable. Open a flagged passage and check whether the pattern fits what the chapter is doing.

Flagged passages

Number of paragraphs meeting the active severity threshold. These are review hints, not errors or FDR results.

deviation

Number of features above the deviation threshold. 0 = none above it; – = no comparison. Not an FDR count or quality rating.

About this comparison

Paragraph values relative to the chapter median: blue = lower, orange = higher. Ring = deviation |z| ≥ 1.5; not a quality rating.

Chapter registers (optional)

Group chapters with a distinct voice (e.g. letters or another narrator). Assigned chapters skip FDR flags while z values still compare against the book. Ranges describe traits, not quality.

Scene targets are set separately in lixity.toml. Analysis filters change the display, not the comparison reference.

Compare versions

The comparison measures the confirmed text, current file and draft with the same settings. If the average falls from 12 to 8, the change is −4 words per sentence. Read the text changes to see whether long sentences were removed or short ones were added. Gaps in saved history indicate unavailable comparable measurements.

Chapter changes are shown only when it is clear which chapter corresponds to which.
